What it really costs to deploy AI in a company.

Why the API price misleads

When a team prices an AI feature, the first number it reaches for is the model's price per million tokens. It is public, precise and easy to multiply. That is exactly why it dominates the conversation, and why it is a poor guide to the total cost.

A token price tells you what one model call costs. A business does not buy model calls. It buys a finished task: a ticket resolved, a contract reviewed, an invoice matched. Between the model call and the finished task sit people, systems and risk. We made a related argument in The token price is not the cost of the task.

The seven cost layers

Think of an AI deployment as seven layers. Only the first one appears on the AI provider's invoice.

  1. Inference. Model calls, including retries, long prompts, retrieved context and the calls you make only for testing.
  2. Integration. Connecting the model to your real systems: CRM, helpdesk, document storage, permissions, logging.
  3. Data preparation. Cleaning, labelling and structuring the documents and examples the system depends on, and keeping them current.
  4. Evaluation and monitoring. Building a test set, measuring quality before launch, and watching for drift after it.
  5. Human review. The people who check, correct or approve outputs. In many deployments this is the largest ongoing cost.
  6. Change management. Training staff, rewriting procedures, and the productivity dip while people adapt.
  7. Cost of errors. What a wrong answer costs you: a refund, a lost customer, a compliance problem. Multiply it by how often it happens.

A worked example: support ticket triage

Example assumptions & scope

This is a constructed teaching example. Volumes, rates, times and error rates are illustrative inputs chosen to show the method. They are not market data, vendor quotes or a customer result.

A mid-sized software company receives 20,000 support tickets a month. It wants an AI system that reads each ticket, tags it, drafts a first reply and routes it to the right team. A support agent checks every draft before it goes out.

Data preparationOne analyst spends 25% of their time keeping examples and macros current, at $6,000 a month$1,500
Evaluation and monitoring10% of one engineer's time, at $12,000 a month$1,200
Human review1 minute per ticket × 20,000 tickets ≈ 333 hours at $25 an hour$8,300
Change managementOne-off $6,000 of training and process work, spread over 24 months$250
Cost of errors1% of tickets misrouted, each costing $10 in extra handling$2,000
Total≈ $14,850

In this example, inference is about 4% of the total. Human review is more than half.

What this means for the decision

The useful question is not "how much do the tokens cost?" but "what did this task cost before, and what does it cost now?"

Suppose that before the AI system, agents spent 5 minutes per ticket on tagging, routing and a first reply. That is roughly 1,667 hours a month, or about $41,700 at the same $25 an hour. With AI drafts plus 1 minute of review, the total in our example is about $14,850. That is a meaningful saving, but it comes from cutting human minutes per task, not from cheap tokens.

The same table shows where the project can fail:

  • If review takes 3 minutes instead of 1, human review rises to about $25,000 a month and most of the saving disappears.
  • If the error rate is 5% instead of 1%, the cost of errors rises to $10,000 a month.
  • A model that costs twice as much per call but halves review time would be the better buy: inference rises by $600, review falls by about $4,150.

A checklist before you approve an AI project

  1. What does the task cost today, per unit, including people's time?
  2. How many human minutes per task remain after AI is introduced?
  3. What is the expected error rate, and what does one error cost?
  4. Who maintains the data and the test set after launch?
  5. Over how many months are the build costs spread?
  6. Which assumption, if it is wrong, kills the business case? Test that one first.

The takeaway

Price AI projects by the cost of a finished task, not by the cost of a model call. The model is the visible part. The system around it is where the budget, and the risk, actually sit.
